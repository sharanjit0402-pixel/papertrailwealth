Here's a money fact that sounds wrong the first time you hear it: **the person who saves less can end up with more.**

Meet two savers.

- **Saver A** starts at **25** and invests **$200 a month** until 65.
- **Saver B** waits until **45**, then invests **$600 a month** until 65. That's three times as much every month.

Who has more money at 65? It's not even close.

> **Quick note:** This article is general education, not investment advice. The numbers below are examples using an assumed average return. Real investments go up and down, and returns are never guaranteed.

## The math: $200 a month vs $600 a month

Let's assume both savers earn a **7% average yearly return**. (More on why we picked 7% below.)

| | Saver A | Saver B |
|---|---|---|
| Starts at | 25 | 45 |
| Monthly amount | $200 | $600 |
| Years investing | 40 | 20 |
| Total money put in | **$96,000** | **$144,000** |
| Value at 65 | **about $525,000** | **about $313,000** |

Saver B paid **$48,000 more** out of pocket and still ended up with about **$212,000 less**.

The difference isn't effort. It isn't income. It's **time**.

## Why time beats money: compound interest in plain English

Compound interest means **your money earns money, and then that money earns money too.**

Say you invest $1,000 and it grows 7% in a year. You now have $1,070. The next year, you earn 7% on $1,070, not just your original $1,000. The year after, you earn 7% on even more.

At first, it feels like nothing is happening. But the growth keeps snowballing, and the biggest jumps come at the end. That's why the last 10 years of Saver A's 40 years do more work than the first 20.

A simple rule of thumb: at 7% a year, money roughly **doubles every 10 years**. Saver A's early dollars get about four doublings before 65. Saver B's dollars get about two.

## The part almost nobody believes

Here's an even stranger example.

- **Saver C** invests $200 a month from **25 to 35**, then **stops completely**. Total put in: **$24,000**.
- **Saver D** waits until **35**, then invests $200 a month for **30 years**, all the way to 65. Total put in: **$72,000**.

At 65, at the same 7% return:

| | Saver C | Saver D |
|---|---|---|
| Years investing | 10, then stopped | 30 |
| Total put in | $24,000 | $72,000 |
| Value at 65 | **about $281,000** | **about $244,000** |

Saver C invested for just 10 years, put in a third of the money, and **still finished ahead.** Those first ten years had three decades to grow.

## What waiting really costs

Here's what $200 a month grows to by 65, depending on when you start (7% average return):

| Start age | Total you put in | Value at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | $96,000 | about $525,000 |
| 30 | $84,000 | about $360,000 |
| 35 | $72,000 | about $244,000 |
| 40 | $60,000 | about $162,000 |
| 45 | $48,000 | about $104,000 |
| 50 | $36,000 | about $63,000 |

Every five years of waiting cuts the final number by roughly a third.

And here's the same idea from the other side: how much you'd need to invest **each month** to reach about **$500,000 by 65**:

| Start age | Monthly amount needed |
|---|---|
| 25 | about $190 |
| 30 | about $280 |
| 35 | about $410 |
| 40 | about $620 |
| 45 | about $960 |
| 50 | about $1,580 |

Starting at 25 costs about the price of a phone bill. Starting at 50 costs about the price of a mortgage.

## Why we used 7%

Over nearly a century, the S&P 500 has grown about **10% a year** on average, before inflation. After inflation, it's closer to **6.5% to 7%**. We used 7% as a middle-of-the-road example.

Some years the market falls a lot. Some years it rises a lot. The 7% is a long-term average, not a promise for any single year, and past returns don't guarantee future ones.

## "I'm not 25 anymore. Is it too late?"

No. The tables show waiting is expensive, but they also show something hopeful: **even starting at 45 or 50, you still end up with far more than you put in.**

A few things many people do to catch up:

- **Get the full 401(k) match.** If your employer matches your contributions, that's an instant return before the market even moves.
- **Use catch-up contributions.** Once you turn 50, the IRS lets you put extra money into 401(k)s and IRAs each year.
- **Raise your savings rate with every raise.** Saving even half of each raise adds up quickly.
- **Start with any amount.** $50 a month now beats $500 a month "someday."

## The bottom line

The best time to start investing was 20 years ago. The second-best time is today.

**The one-line lesson:** In investing, time is worth more than money. You can always earn more money. You can never earn back more time.
