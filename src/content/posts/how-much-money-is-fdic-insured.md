In March 1933, almost every bank in America was locked. Millions of families had no idea if their life savings still existed. More than 9,000 banks had failed in just four years, and people who were last in line at the bank often got nothing at all.

That disaster is the reason your money has a safety net today. It's called **FDIC insurance**, and most people have never been told how it actually works.

Here's the short version: **if your bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per person, per bank, for each type of account ownership.** Let's break down what that really means.

> **Quick note:** This article is general education, not financial advice. For your exact situation, use the FDIC's own tools (linked below) or talk to your bank.

## What is the FDIC?

The **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** is an independent U.S. government agency created in 1933, during the Great Depression. Its job is simple: if an insured bank fails, it makes sure depositors get their money back, up to the insurance limit.

A few things most people don't know:

- **You don't sign up.** If your bank is FDIC-insured, your eligible deposits are covered automatically.
- **You don't pay for it.** Banks pay insurance premiums to the FDIC, not customers.
- **It has never failed.** Since the FDIC started in 1934, no depositor has lost a single penny of insured deposits.

The very first person ever paid back was **Lydia Lobsiger**, a 66-year-old widow from East Peoria, Illinois. When her small bank collapsed in 1934, she received a check for $1,250, her entire life savings. Back then, the limit was $2,500. Today, it's 100 times higher.

## The $250,000 rule, explained

The standard limit is **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category.** Each part of that sentence matters.

**1. Per depositor.** The limit applies to each person, not each account. If you have a checking account and a savings account in your name at the same bank, they're added together. $150,000 in checking plus $150,000 in savings is $300,000, so **$50,000 would be uninsured.**

**2. Per insured bank.** Coverage is separate at each bank. $250,000 at Bank A and $250,000 at Bank B is fully insured. But be careful: different "brands" can belong to the same bank, so check the bank's legal name.

**3. Per ownership category.** This is the part that can multiply your coverage. The FDIC treats different types of ownership as separate buckets, each with its own limit.

## The main ownership categories

| Account type | How much is covered |
| --- | --- |
| **Single accounts** (in one person's name) | $250,000 per owner |
| **Joint accounts** (two or more owners) | $250,000 per co-owner, so $500,000 for a couple |
| **Certain retirement accounts** (like IRAs held as bank deposits) | $250,000 per owner |
| **Trust accounts** (including payable-on-death accounts) | $250,000 per beneficiary, up to five beneficiaries ($1.25 million per owner) |
| **Business accounts** (corporations, partnerships) | $250,000 per business |

### Example: how a married couple can be covered for $1.5 million at one bank

- Husband's single account: **$250,000**
- Wife's single account: **$250,000**
- Joint account: **$500,000**
- Husband's IRA deposits: **$250,000**
- Wife's IRA deposits: **$250,000**

**Total insured at one bank: $1.5 million**, without even counting trust accounts. The exact rules have details, so always confirm with the FDIC's calculator before relying on this.

## What the FDIC covers, and what it doesn't

**Covered** (deposit products at an FDIC-insured bank):

- Checking accounts
- Savings accounts
- Money market *deposit* accounts
- Certificates of deposit (CDs)
- Cashier's checks and money orders issued by the bank

**Not covered**, even if you bought them through your bank:

- Stocks, bonds and mutual funds
- Money market *mutual funds*
- Annuities and life insurance
- Crypto
- The contents of a safe deposit box

Investments can lose value, and the FDIC doesn't protect against that. Brokerage accounts have a separate protection called SIPC, which covers you if the *brokerage firm itself* fails, not if your investments drop.

## What about credit unions?

Credit unions aren't covered by the FDIC. Instead, federally insured credit unions are covered by the **NCUA** (National Credit Union Administration), with the same standard $250,000 limit and the same backing of the U.S. government.

## What happens if your bank actually fails?

Bank failures are rare, but they still happen. In March 2023, customers of **Silicon Valley Bank** pulled out **$42 billion in a single day** using their phones. By the next morning, the bank had been shut down.

When a bank fails, the FDIC usually does one of two things:

1. **Moves your accounts to a healthy bank**, often over a weekend. You might just get a new debit card and keep banking as normal.
2. **Pays you directly** for your insured balance, typically within a few business days.

Money above the insurance limit is **not guaranteed**. Depositors may get some of it back later as the failed bank's assets are sold, but it can take time, and there's no promise of getting all of it.

## One modern trap: banking apps

Many popular money apps aren't banks. They partner with banks behind the scenes. Your money may be "eligible" for FDIC insurance, but only if it's actually held at an insured partner bank and the records are kept properly. If the app company itself fails, getting your money back can take a long time. Read the fine print, and know which bank actually holds your money.

## How to check if your money is insured

It takes less than a minute:

- **Check your bank:** use the FDIC's [BankFind tool](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind) to confirm your bank is insured.
- **Check your coverage:** use the FDIC's [Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator (EDIE)](https://edie.fdic.gov/) to see exactly how much of your money is covered.
- **For credit unions:** use the NCUA's [credit union locator](https://mapping.ncua.gov/).

## The bottom line

Your grandparents learned about bank safety the hard way, standing outside a locked bank in 1933. That's why so many families kept cash in a coffee can for decades.

Today, you don't have to. For most people, every dollar in the bank is protected, as long as you stay under the limits and understand how ownership categories work.

**A bank holds your money. Trust holds the bank.** And since 1934, the FDIC has made sure that trust holds.
