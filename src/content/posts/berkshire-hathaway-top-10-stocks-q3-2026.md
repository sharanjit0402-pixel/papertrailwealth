Berkshire Hathaway, the company Warren Buffett spent 60 years building, has **$304.2 billion** invested in U.S. stocks. And it's not spread across hundreds of companies. **Just 10 stocks make up about 92% of it.**

We know this because of one government form: the **13F**. Every big investment manager in America has to file one with the SEC every quarter, listing the U.S. stocks it owns. Berkshire filed its latest one on **November 16, 2026**, showing what it held on September 30.

Here's the full top 10, what changed, and what regular investors can actually learn from it.

> **Quick note:** This article is general education, not investment advice. We're explaining what Berkshire owns, not telling you to buy it.

## Who's making these decisions now?

This is the third 13F since **Greg Abel** took over as CEO. Warren Buffett retired as chief executive at the end of 2025, and Abel took the job in January 2026. Portfolio manager Ted Weschler also helps run the stock portfolio. The filing doesn't say which of them bought or sold each stock.

So when you see headlines like "Warren Buffett is buying…", be careful. It's Buffett's company and Buffett's style, but in 2026 it's Abel's team making the calls.

## What Berkshire bought in Q3 2026

- **Bending Spoons (BSP), a brand-new position.** Berkshire bought about 20.7 million shares of the Italian software company, worth $689 million at the end of September, and added more in October for a stake of about 4%. It was the only new stock of the quarter.
- **Lennar (LEN), roughly doubled.** Berkshire's stake in the homebuilder about doubled, and after more buying in October it owns about 12.5% of the company.
- **Delta Air Lines (DAL), up 12%.** The stake grew to about 64.5 million shares, worth around $5.4 billion.
- **Macy's (M), up slightly.** A small increase, to about 7.5 million shares.

## What Berkshire sold in Q3 2026

- **Bank of America (BAC), down 6%.** Berkshire sold about 27.5 million shares, worth roughly $1.5 billion, the biggest sale of the quarter. It has been cutting this stake steadily since mid-2024, when it owned more than a billion shares.
- **Capital One (COF), down 62%.**
- **VeriSign (VRSN), down 34%.**
- **Nucor (NUE), down 15%,** and **Kroger (KR), down 10%.**

**What didn't change:** Apple, American Express, Alphabet and Coca-Cola. The top holdings were left alone. Apple's value rose from $66 billion to $75.9 billion simply because the stock price went up, not because Berkshire bought more.

Overall, Berkshire bought **$2.37 billion** of stocks and sold **$2.79 billion**, so it was a small net seller for the quarter.

## The biggest move isn't in the 13F at all

Here's the part most headlines miss. In the same quarter, Berkshire spent a **record $9.6 billion buying back its own shares**. It's also sitting on more than **$350 billion in cash and Treasury bills**.

In plain English: the company's managers thought their own stock was the best deal around, and they're keeping a huge pile of cash ready for whenever prices get cheap.

## What regular investors can learn from this

You don't need $300 billion to borrow a few of Berkshire's habits:

1. **Concentration in what you understand.** Berkshire owns businesses most people use every day: iPhones, credit cards, Google, Coke. It doesn't need 500 stocks it can't explain.
2. **Patience.** Coca-Cola has been in the portfolio since the late 1980s. The biggest wins came from holding great businesses for decades, not trading them every week.
3. **Cash is a tool.** Berkshire's huge cash pile means it never has to sell in a panic. For a regular family, that's what an emergency fund does.
4. **Most people don't need to pick stocks at all.** Buffett himself has said for years that most people are better off in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. Many of the companies above are already in one.

**The one-line lesson:** The world's most famous investor didn't win by owning everything. He won by owning a few great things for a very long time.

## FAQ

**What is a 13F?**
A quarterly report that big investment managers (with more than $100 million in U.S. stocks) must file with the SEC. It lists the U.S.-listed stocks they owned on the last day of the quarter.

**Is the 13F up to date?**
Not exactly. It's filed up to 45 days after the quarter ends, so it shows what Berkshire owned on September 30, not today. Berkshire may have bought or sold since.

**Should I copy Berkshire's portfolio?**
That's a personal decision, and this isn't advice. Keep in mind the filing is old by the time you see it, and Berkshire's goals, taxes and timeline are very different from yours.
