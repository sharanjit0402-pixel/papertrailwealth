An IRA is one of the best tools you have for retirement. But the first question trips everyone up: **Roth or Traditional?**

The good news: the difference comes down to one simple idea. **Do you want to pay tax now, or later?**

> **Quick note:** This article is general education, not tax or investment advice. Your situation may be different, so check with a tax professional if you're unsure.

## The one-sentence difference

- **Traditional IRA:** You may get a tax break **now**, and you pay tax when you take the money out in retirement.
- **Roth IRA:** You get **no** tax break now, but your money can come out **tax-free** in retirement, growth included.

Same account idea. Opposite timing.

## Side by side

| | Roth IRA | Traditional IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Tax break today? | No | Often yes (may be deductible) |
| Tax when you withdraw in retirement? | No, if rules are met | Yes, taxed as income |
| 2026 contribution limit | $7,500 ($8,600 if 50+) | $7,500 ($8,600 if 50+) |
| Income limit to contribute? | Yes | No (but the deduction can be limited) |
| Required withdrawals later? | No, for the original owner | Yes, starting at age 73 |
| Take out your contributions early? | Yes, anytime, tax- and penalty-free | Usually a 10% penalty plus tax before 59½ |

The **$7,500** limit is shared. If you have both kinds of IRA, your total across both can't go over it.

## The 2026 income rules

**Roth IRA:** You can contribute the full amount if your income (technically your "modified adjusted gross income") is below:

- **$153,000** if you're single
- **$242,000** if you're married filing jointly

Above that, the amount you can put in shrinks. At **$168,000** (single) or **$252,000** (married), you can't contribute directly at all.

**Traditional IRA:** Anyone with earned income can contribute. But if you **or your spouse** have a retirement plan at work, like a 401(k), your tax deduction starts to shrink at higher incomes. For 2026, if you're single and covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out between **$81,000 and $91,000** of income.

## Which one fits you?

### A Roth IRA may fit you if...

- **You're early in your career.** Your income (and tax rate) is likely to be higher later, so paying tax now at a lower rate can make sense.
- **You want flexibility.** You can take out what you put in (not the growth) at any time without tax or penalty. That makes it a nice backup for emergencies.
- **You like the idea of tax-free retirement money.** Every dollar of growth can be yours to keep.

### A Traditional IRA may fit you if...

- **You're in a high tax bracket today** and expect to be in a lower one in retirement.
- **You want to lower this year's tax bill.** The deduction can mean a bigger refund now.
- **Your income is too high for a Roth.**

### Can't decide?

Many people simply split it: some money in each. That gives you a mix of tax-free and taxable money in retirement, so you're not betting everything on what tax rates will be decades from now.

## A quick example

Say you're 25 and put **$300 a month** into an IRA until age 65, earning an average of 7% a year. You'd contribute **$144,000**, and it could grow to roughly **$787,000**.

- In a **Roth**, that whole amount could come out **tax-free**.
- In a **Traditional**, you'd have gotten tax breaks along the way, but withdrawals would be **taxed as income** in retirement.

Either way, the biggest win is the same: **starting.** ([See what waiting 10 years costs.](/blog/start-investing-at-25-vs-45/))

## How to open one (about 15 minutes)

1. **Pick a brokerage.** Big names like Fidelity, Charles Schwab and Vanguard offer IRAs with no account fees.
2. **Choose Roth or Traditional** when you open the account.
3. **Add money.** Even $50 is a real start. You have until the tax deadline in April 2027 to make 2026 contributions.
4. **Invest it.** This is the step people forget. Money sitting in an IRA as cash doesn't grow much. Many beginners choose a low-cost [index fund](/blog/index-funds-for-beginners/) or a target-date fund.
5. **Set up automatic monthly contributions** so you never have to think about it.

## Common mistakes

- **Opening the account but never investing the money.** Check that your cash is actually invested.
- **Going over the limit.** Extra contributions can trigger a penalty. Keep your total across all IRAs at or below $7,500 ($8,600 if 50+).
- **Ignoring the Roth income limit.** If your income is close to the limit, double-check before contributing.
- **Skipping your 401(k) match.** If your job offers a match, get the full match first. It's free money.

## The bottom line

Roth or Traditional is a smaller decision than it looks. **Not opening an IRA at all is the real mistake.**

**The one-line lesson:** You will pay the tax either way. The only choice is when.
