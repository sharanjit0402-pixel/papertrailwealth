On Thursday, November 19, 2026, Anthropic, the company behind the AI assistant Claude, started trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker **ANT**. It was the **largest IPO on record**, raising about **$81.3 billion**.

The headlines said the stock **rose 17%** on its first day. But many people who bought that day were **losing money by the closing bell**.

Both things are true. To see why, you need to understand how an IPO actually works.

## What is an IPO?

An **IPO (initial public offering)** is the moment a private company first sells shares to the public and lists them on a stock exchange. Before an IPO, ownership is mostly limited to founders, employees and private investors. After it, anyone with a brokerage account can buy and sell the shares.

Companies do it mainly to **raise money** to grow, and to give early owners a way to eventually sell their shares.

## The two prices that confuse everyone

An IPO has **two different prices**, and the news usually reports only one of them.

### 1. The IPO price (set the night before)

The company and its banks decide the **IPO price**, also called the offer price, the evening before trading starts. They base it on how much demand they saw from big investors during the "roadshow," a series of pitches to funds and institutions.

For Anthropic, the planned price range was **$70 to $78** a share. Demand was so strong that the final IPO price was set higher, at **$83**.

The people who get shares at the IPO price are **mostly large funds and institutions**, plus a limited number of customers at some brokerages. Most everyday investors never get shares at this price.

### 2. The opening price (set by the market)

The next morning, the stock starts trading on the exchange. The **opening price** isn't chosen by the company. It's set by matching all the buy and sell orders waiting to go.

When a lot of people want in, the first trade can come in far above the IPO price. Anthropic's first trade didn't happen until about **11:48 a.m. Eastern**, and it came in at **$111.50**, roughly **34% above** the $83 IPO price.

## What happened during the day

| Moment | Price | Compared to $83 IPO price |
|---|---|---|
| IPO price (set Nov. 18) | $83.00 | — |
| First trade | $111.50 | +34% |
| Day's high | about $115 | about +39% |
| Day's low | about $92.70 | about +12% |
| **Closing price** | **$97.17** | **+17%** |

So here's how two buyers ended the same day:

- **Got shares at $83:** up **$14.17 a share**, about **+17%**.
- **Bought at the $111.50 opening trade:** down **$14.33 a share**, about **−13%**.

Same company. Same day. Opposite results.

## Why "the stock rose 17%" can be misleading

When the news says an IPO "jumped" on day one, it's almost always measured from the **IPO price**, a price most regular investors never had a chance to pay. **Your real starting point is whatever you pay once trading begins.** On a hyped first day, that price is set by the most excited buyers in the market.

## History has seen this before

A big first-day move doesn't tell you where a stock goes next. A few well-known examples:

- **Facebook (2012)** ended its first day barely above its IPO price, then fell about **54%** within its first six months as a public company.
- **Uber (2019)** closed its first day **below** its IPO price.
- **Google (2004)** rose about **18%** on day one and went on to become one of the most valuable companies in the world.

The first day is one data point. It isn't a prediction.

## Three terms worth knowing

- **Underwriters:** the investment banks that help a company set its IPO price and sell the first shares.
- **IPO pop:** the jump from the IPO price to the first trading price.
- **Lock-up period:** a stretch of time, often several months, when insiders and early investors agree not to sell their shares. When lock-ups end, more shares can hit the market at once, which can affect the price.

## The lesson

An IPO is really two events: a private sale at a set price, mostly to big investors, and then a public auction the next morning. Knowing the difference helps you read the headlines correctly.

**The one-line lesson:** A stock's big debut can be a win for the people who got in early, and a trap for the people who got in excited.
