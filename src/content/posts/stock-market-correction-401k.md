If you opened your 401(k) this week and felt your stomach drop, you're not alone. U.S. stocks have lost about **$6 trillion in 45 days**, according to The Kobeissi Letter. That works out to roughly $225 billion a day, for 31 trading days in a row.

On Friday, November 20, the S&P 500 fell 3.7% and officially entered a **correction**. The Nasdaq dropped more than 5% in a single day and wiped out all of its gains for 2026.

So what does that actually mean for your retirement money? Let's break it down in plain English.

> **Quick note:** This article is general education, not investment advice. Everyone's situation is different. If you're making a big decision about your retirement, talk to a licensed financial professional.

## What is a stock market correction?

A **correction** is when a major index, like the S&P 500, falls **10% or more** from its most recent high. If the drop reaches **20%**, it's called a **bear market**.

The S&P 500 last hit a record on **October 6, 2026**. By November 20, it had closed more than 10% below that peak. That's the line that made this a correction.

## What caused this one?

Several things piled up over six weeks:

- **Worries about AI spending.** Big tech companies are pouring hundreds of billions of dollars into AI data centers, and investors started asking when that money pays off.
- **The midterm elections.** Many newly elected candidates have been critical of data-center growth, which spooked AI investors.
- **Nvidia's turmoil.** CEO Jensen Huang resigned on November 12. On November 20, reports of an SEC accounting probe sent the stock down 14% in one day. Nvidia alone has lost more than $2.5 trillion in market value since early October.
- **Inflation and the Fed.** Hotter-than-expected inflation has some Fed officials talking about raising interest rates again.
- **The Anthropic IPO.** The biggest IPO ever was expected to lift the mood. Instead, its shares slid after day one.

## Where did $6 trillion actually go?

Here's the part most people never hear: **most of it was never cash in the first place.**

A company's "market value" is just the last price someone paid for one share, multiplied by every share that exists. When nervous sellers accept a lower price, *every* share gets marked down, including all the shares nobody sold.

Here's a simple example:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Last trade price | $100 | $80 |
| Shares in existence | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| "Market value" | $100,000 | $80,000 |
| Shares actually sold | | 10 |

Only 10 shares changed hands, but the whole company's value "lost" $20,000. That money didn't go to anyone. The price simply changed.

That's why a drop in your 401(k) is a **paper loss**. It becomes a real, locked-in loss only if you sell at the lower price.

## What history says happens next

Corrections are more common than most people think. Since 1950, the S&P 500 has had **nearly 40 corrections**, about one every two years on average, based on data from Yardeni Research.

A few patterns stand out:

1. **Every correction since 1950 has eventually recovered.** Some took months, a few took years.
2. **Most corrections don't become bear markets.** Only a minority of 10% drops went on to fall 20% or more.
3. **The worst days and the best days often come close together.** Some of the market's biggest one-day gains have happened during scary stretches.

History isn't a promise. No one knows if this drop ends at 10%, 20% or somewhere else. But it is a pattern worth knowing before you react.

## What 401(k) savers commonly do during a correction

Again, this isn't advice. These are the habits many long-term savers stick to, and the reasons behind them:

- **Keep contributing.** If you're still putting money in every paycheck, lower prices mean each dollar buys more shares. This is called dollar-cost averaging.
- **Don't check the balance every day.** Daily swings feel much bigger than they are over 20 or 30 years.
- **Check your mix, not the headlines.** If you're close to retirement, it's worth making sure your mix of stocks and bonds still fits your timeline. Many target-date funds adjust this automatically.
- **Keep an emergency fund.** Having cash set aside means you won't be forced to sell investments at a low point. (Read: [How to Build an Emergency Fund From $0](/blog/emergency-fund-from-zero/).)
- **Get the free match.** If your employer matches contributions, many savers make sure they don't stop contributing and give up that free money.

## The bottom line

Six trillion dollars sounds like money that vanished into thin air. Most of it was never cash, just prices on a screen that moved when confidence moved.

**The one-line lesson:** A falling price is a feeling. Selling is a decision. Know the difference before you act.
